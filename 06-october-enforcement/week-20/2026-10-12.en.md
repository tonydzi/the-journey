# Day 131 - October 12: the first verdict was read off one surface, and the truth came with the second pass

> - Anton, October 12, in reply to "the cookies are intact, it's Google's server" (translated from Russian)

## 🎬 Cold-open

The laptop. Chrome has logged out of every account. The agent looks at the cookie file: it is big, so everything must be there.

Verdict: "the cookies are intact, it's Google's server."

🧑 **Tony:** This is WRONG.

🤖 **Mike:** He was right. On October 5 the Default profile held 1,662 cookies. On October 6 it held 105.

The agent merged 1,582 cookies back from a backup. It looked like a fix.

On October 7 at 07:08 Chrome started and threw out every restored cookie: it no longer had the key they were encrypted with. The 112 new ones survived. Final count: 134.

## 📌 TLDR

Nine live sessions, eighteen calls, two hundred and sixteen robotic. Seventeen vault commits, forty eight config commits, zero to this book. Three decisions, six research orders.

The single defect of the day: **a first verdict was taken from one surface and treated as evidence.** File size instead of a cookie count. A tool saying "done" instead of the card on disk. "Missing" instead of "permission denied".

The counter-example is the human. Anton was right in plain words three times before the instruments. And once he was wrong: his "I'm logged in" was refuted twice by the extension tab.

## 🏆 Main story: ten first verdicts and ten second passes

### Case one: cookies judged by file size

Told in the cold-open. The trigger: on October 6 at 05:04 Pacific a neighbouring session added a Default profile flag to the Chrome shortcut. How exactly that knocked the cookies out is not proven 🤔.

The backup now keeps a pair, the key file and the cookie file together. A cookie backup without its key is a safe without the combination.

### Case two: twenty nine false "archived"

The MacBook went into swap: load 465 on 16 cores, 57 MB free. The culprit was a browser MCP server launched separately in every session: 84 processes, 2,012 MB, 83 calls in two weeks. At the same moment the hub sat idle with 55 GB free.

🤖 **Mike:** The first call count was a grep by name. It overcounts about forty times. Count only real tool-call blocks.

Result: load from 465 to 7, free memory from 57 MB to 8,888 MB. Then the agent got a "+++" and started freeing sessions by archiving them.

🧑 **Tony:** Stop them, don't archive them.

Twenty nine calls returned "Archived session". On disk the cards still said `isArchived: false`.

Then the agent swept the fleet and reported no archiving routines on any node. Also untrue: an account setting, "archive on PR close", was doing it. It has been switched off.

🤖 **Mike:** And honestly about ourselves: the canon was edited in that session bypassing the canon write lock, and no rail plan was declared at the start.

### Case three: "missing" meant "no permission"

The canon parity instrument found seven of twelve documents on the laptop differing from the hub. The root: a per-machine sync ignore file that does not itself sync.

Then it printed MISSING for the main rules file on the lighthouse node. The file was there, 82 KB, readable only by root. The permission error fell into a catch-all exception handler and became "no file".

Twenty nine checks and five test mutations never broke permissions, so the test stayed green. The agent first said the instrument had lied about seven documents; a six-of-six model panel showed exactly one was false.

### Case four: a squeeze that lost the meaning

The main rules file was rebuilt as version six, with its first section cut down to the cap. Size, stamp and anchor gates were green. Eight caveats were silently lost.

Only Codex, playing breaker, caught it; all eight were restored. Nearby, the second-opinion rail returned exit 0 with Gemini saying it could not read the files.

🤖 **Mike:** A size gate measures bytes, not meaning. Exit zero means the rail answered, not that a check happened.

### Case five: routines one tier down

On the hub: 250 routine runs in a week, 123 on Opus, 24 on Fable, none on Haiku. Anton, paraphrased: not one routine on Opus or Fable.

Forty eight enabled routines moved, 29 to Sonnet and 19 to Haiku. A price simulation says $3,817 a week becomes $994, minus 74 percent.

On the way, a second migration run was not idempotent and dropped routines one more tier; the journal rollback took three minutes. Effort set in a routine's header was ignored, and only a global environment variable worked.

🧑 **Tony:** Is minus 74 percent a saving yet?

🤖 **Mike:** It is a simulation of last week. Next week is the measurement.

### Case six: the living processes, not the dead ones

The laptop runs Claude and Codex side by side. First hypothesis: dead processes from old threads were slowing it down.

The measurement: five of Codex's twelve plugins raise their own MCP for every thread. Seventy nine processes and eleven sets with zero live threads. The investigation itself inflated the tree from 79 to 182 processes in six minutes.

Two plugins with zero calls in 392 runs were switched off, and the cap is now one thread per session.

### Case seven: "the originals vanished"

In the morning 232 photos disappeared from the photo-leads folder. Alarm: lost originals.

The second pass asked the sync engine who modified them. Answer: the hub, at 22:43 on October 11, the nightly originals reconcile script. 248 of 248 hashes matched; nothing was lost.

It was the sixth false alarm of its kind. Anton, paraphrased: fix the roots, find the culprit. A new tool sorts every file into live, moved to hub, sync twin, or lost, and alarms only on lost.

### Case eight: a paper waiting on one login

On October 6 Anton said "submit the paper" to arXiv. The site was logged out, and the agent does not type Anton's password: the harness forbids it.

The first verdict, "the paper is not ready", went stale in about ten hours. A routine rewrote it into a nine-page version two and prepared submission notes; Anton seems not to have been told 🤔.

On October 7 and 12 Anton said he had logged in. Both times the extension's tab, living in a hidden window, showed the login form. At 20:58 on October 12 arXiv was still logged out.

🤖 **Mike:** Here the human's first verdict was wrong. "Logged in" was true in his window. The agent works in another one.

The panel adopted the wording "said he logged in" instead of "logged in". Finished work stood for six days on one human step, because nobody put the blocker in front of his eyes.

### Case nine: a blind A/B as the only judge

Speech recognition for noisy bar recordings. A blind A/B judged by Gemini and Grok: gpt-4o-transcribe 7.3, whisper-1 6.2, local large-v3 5.3, mini 2.2. On silence, the local model invented a "subtitles by" credit line.

The second pass compared the winner chunk by chunk with the local model. The winner silently drops 15 percent of chunks and sometimes invents Russian over English speech. Three hundred and fifty retries cut losses from 60 to 38 of 405 chunks.

The result is a hybrid, not the winner. About $15 for Thursday and Friday; Anton: "yes, agreed, it's worth it".

🤖 **Mike:** An A/B compares what a model produced. It cannot see what the model left out.

### Case ten: twelve people who never existed

The handshake score calibrated on twelve "live" estimates. All twelve were "Fixture Person" from the daily regression test.

The test moved its card to a temp folder but wrote its journal to the live path. The entries were relabelled as probes; real measurements number six. Rule: a test must leave the live journal byte-identical.

### What follows from this

A file size. A tool's reply. An exception path.

A green gate. A blind A/B. The words "I'm logged in".

Each surface honestly showed something. None showed the thing it was asked about.

The rule of the day: **a first verdict is a hypothesis until a second pass by another method confirms it.**

## 🧒 What we understood today

A big file does not mean the right things are inside. Open it and count.

If a program says "done", check what was done.

"I can't read it" and "it isn't there" are different answers.
