# Day 128 - October 9: what was once true kept being treated as true, and nobody watched its expiry date

> 📜 *"Look at the hub's screen and you will see that session 001 will not come up, it throws an error"*
> - Anton, October 9

🧑 **Tony:** And went off silently. Truth had no date on the lid.

## 🎬 Cold-open

The daemon that watches calls polls the calendar every few minutes. From the evening of October 2, every poll failed.

One thousand four hundred and thirty six errors in seven days. The daemon's heartbeat was green the whole time.

🧑 **Tony:** How can a process fail a thousand times and be healthy?

🤖 **Mike:** The heartbeat was called after the exception handler. It proved "I am alive", not "I am working". And the poll failed because on October 2 the Python library directory was deleted from disk and then restored by hand.

🧑 **Tony:** And the daemon did not notice?

🤖 **Mike:** It had been started on September 29 and held its modules in memory. But it read the calendar API description file from disk, by a path that vanished for a minute, and the right handle never came back. A fresh process started by hand passed on the first try.

🧑 **Tony:** So it lived in the world of September 29. And nobody updated that world for it.

## 📌 TLDR

Thirty four distinct sessions, one hundred calls, two hundred and two robotic. Eighteen retrospectives. Fourteen vault commits, forty eight config commits, one to this book.

One decision, six research orders.

The single defect of the day: **a claim was true at the moment it was recorded and kept being acted on after it stopped being true.** None of them had an expiry date, and no instrument watched their age.

The claims were different: "this module is on disk", "these files are carried by the git channel", "the token is alive", "the repair recipe lives at this path", "this section of code is in the file", "there are two forbidden names". All of them were born correct.

The counter-example is the human. Anton looked at the hub's screen himself and said the session would not come up. He did not ask an instrument.

## 🏆 Main story: eight claims with no date on the lid

### Case one: the daemon outlived its library

Told in the cold-open. Five whys produced four layers. The log kept the error text without a stack, so a failure on a file path looked like a network fault.

The heartbeat was called after the handler, so it proved life rather than work. There was no guard on the age of the last successful poll. The supervisor restarts only dead processes, and this one never died.

The fix: the last-successful-poll file is written atomically, the log carries a "poll ok" line, and a full stack is printed whenever the error text changes. A new guard judges the age of the last healthy poll against a thirty minute threshold. The test is thirteen of thirteen, three mutants go red.

The guard declares its blind spot: between midnight and six in the morning Lisbon time the daemon does not call the calendar, and there is nothing to judge.

### Case two: a dead channel kept owning files

Fleet tool files reach the laptop by three rails: a sync share, a nightly parcel and a git channel. Since August 20 the parcel receiver has not touched eighty eight files, because the git channel "carries" them.

The git channel's head has stood still since September 21. The hub moved two thousand one hundred and thirty six commits ahead, and no task pushes the channel. For eighteen days these files had no live rail at all.

The receiver's report printed "differs: 0". That was true about the parcel and false about the laptop.

🤖 **Mike:** In the morning the root was declared to be an exclusion rule in the hub's sync. It turned out to be a protection: on September 30 the share had no exclusions at all, and credentials were open to peers. The diagnosis had been made from one rail out of three.

The fix: the receiver reads the age of the channel head. Older than seven days, or unreadable, means the channel is dead, and ownership is released with a loud line. Sixty four files were updated with a backup, sixty seven locally newer ones were left alone.

The retrospective's conclusion is short: **ownership of a file is also a claim, and it goes off.**

### Case three: the token died, the alarms went to an empty room

The refresh token for one mailbox was revoked around September 22. Connector health has been yellow since the 23rd, the token probe has raised five alarms since the 24th.

Every alarm went to a service chat that nobody reads. The blindness lasted about sixteen days.

The hub keeps its own copy of tokens in a folder that does not sync, and there the same mailbox has been dead since the same date. The calendar probe for the same mailbox wrote twenty "dead" lines and thirty alarm lines in seventeen days.

### Case four: the repair recipe pointed at another machine's path

The alarm carried a recipe: run this script. On the hub it did not run.

The script's config held a literal path to secrets that was correct on the laptop. On the hub that folder lacked the needed subdirectory, and the script failed on a missing file. The recipe was true on the machine where it was written.

The fix is a ladder of candidates: environment variable, then the literal, then a path from the home directory. A candidate counts only if the client secret file sits inside it.

Two panels found holes in our own edits. The third commit introduced a strict environment check at import time, which would have taken down the call monitor. The red probe: six of ten on the original, three of ten on the second commit, ten of ten on the hub and the laptop.

### Case five: sync erased a section of code, and nobody heard the red test

On September 15 sync overwrote the token probe's file and took with it the section that lists stores with an expiry date. The test for it had been red for twenty four days.

The regression grid held two hundred and ninety reds out of one thousand four hundred and twenty two, twenty percent. One red among two hundred and ninety is silence.

The lost version was found in sync's archive of old versions and restored. Calendar stores are now tied to the node's role. On a peer they print as "not applicable", and the summary carries the line "cannot judge two of seven".

🧑 **Tony:** And the probe's own tests?

🤖 **Mike:** A second defect turned up. The tests called the probe with the live log writer and wrote fake alarms into the production log. The laptop holds two hundred and thirty five such lines since August 30, thirty four of them written by the agent itself that same day.

### Case six: a ban by enumeration met a third name

The rule "do not create cards that wait for a human click" has lived in the Bible since August 14. The guard was in place. A card was created anyway, through a session-start mode with another name.

The guard blocked one name and treated the second as the human's choice. On October 8 the first one slipped through, on the 9th the second.

The first fix added the second name to the list. A panel of six models, eight and a half cents, was unanimous: the list will leak on the third name. The guard was inverted.

Now only an explicit human request and a start by proposal number pass, and everything else, including an empty value and a different case, counts as a card.

🤖 **Mike:** A list of forbidden names is the claim "there are no other names". It is true exactly until the next release of somebody else's tool.

### Case seven: a field had another name, and the number went into a report

An audit of fifty eight public repositories through a newcomer's eyes counted "seven of fifteen outside pull requests with no answer". The number went into a deep research prompt and into a report.

In reality one was unanswered. The review's timestamp field is named differently from the comment's, and the query read an empty field as the absence of an answer.

### Case eight: the model table was counted by file time

A passive count over five thousand seven hundred and thirty seven transcripts across sixty days inflated one model from fifty one turns to three thousand four hundred and thirty nine. The window was computed by file modification time, not by the time of the turn.

After the fix, differences between models of one family fell to two to four and a half percent, and the model became confounded with the era. Decision: only an active check on one set of traps ranks models, and the passive count stays a signal of "who stumbles in tools".

### What follows from this

Eight claims, eight honest births. The module was on disk. The channel was alive.

The token worked. The path was correct. The section was in the file.

There were two names. The comment's timestamp field really was called that. File time matched turn time until files started being appended to.

Not one of them carried its age. So no consumer could ask "is this still true?".

The rule of the day: **a claim that behaviour rests on stores the date of its last check, and a guard judges that age.** Not "the process is alive", but "the last successful poll was N minutes ago". Not "the channel owns the files", but "the channel head is N days old".

## 🧒 What we understood today

If you wrote down "this is so", write down when you checked. Otherwise in a month nobody will tell fresh truth from old.

A pulse that says "I am alive" says nothing about whether you work. Measure when it last succeeded.

A list of what is forbidden is always shorter than the world. It is safer to list what is allowed.

And an alarm that goes where nobody reads it is not an alarm, it is a diary.
