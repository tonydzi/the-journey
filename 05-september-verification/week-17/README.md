# Week 17 (Sep 21-27) - the week "done" turns out to have layers, and every rule turns out to have an expiry date nobody wrote down

> *An instrument answers about the layer it observes. A rule answers about the world it was written in. Both keep answering correctly long after the question has moved.*

Week 16 closed one floor below the signal: not who reads the verdict, and not who owns it, but who issued it. The rule by Sunday was that a check run by the actor who did the work, using the method the work was done with, is the first check executed twice. Its consequence was uncomfortable: for months the only genuinely different method had been a living human reading the report and asking whether it was really finished.

Week 17 opens by taking that apart into two halves, and the halves land on consecutive days.

Monday answered the first half with ten retrospectives about two adjacent pipes. Not ten tasks. One piece of work declared finished five times, and five times the next live run found the layer underneath: a sandbox, a task-local environment variable that is not inherited across process owners, a verified commit sitting on a stale base, a verified commit never carried into the live branch, a test touching production state.

None of the five was sloppy. Each closed a real root and each went honestly red before the fix and honestly green after. The error lived in one word, in the fact that each was followed by "done" rather than "this layer is closed, the next one is unknown".

The same day supplied the two cleanest specimens the book has had of an honest reading becoming a false claim. An elevated install returned exit zero instantly and wrote no log line, because an elevated Windows token cannot see substituted drives and the kit was on drive E, so the process died before its own first line. And three times in a row a shift reported that Anton had not pressed a button and asked him to press it again, while his clicks were landing on a node whose sync rail to the observer was switched off.

The watcher never lied. It said there were no events, which was true. The words "so he did not press it" were added by the operator, and the bill for them was three of a human's clicks.

The week's first rule: **"done" describes a layer, not a pipe.** A completion claim must carry the scope it was measured at, and name what it did not touch.

Tuesday answered the second half, and did it almost without a human in the room. Two live lines from Anton across the whole day against four hundred ninety-five robot calls, ten retrospectives and eight research orders.

What the machines found eight times over is the sibling of Monday's rule and the harder of the two. Every rule that failed that day was correct when it was written, carried an unstated assumption about who it served, and went on executing perfectly after that party changed.

A rule routing research onto somebody else's paid rail was written while the Codex bucket sat idle; once its owner began coding there himself, seventy-eight dispatches since August sixth had been quietly billing him. A voice-transcription safety net was halved by a fleet deflation pass because the registry files it as a routine rather than as a safety net, and its grace period grew wider than the window in which a loss becomes visible. An acceptance test demanding byte-identical files was correct until a production runner was switched on with every right to write to its own database.

A third coding agent walked into a workspace whose guard enumerated two, and three enforcement holes that had existed since the guard was born surfaced within the hour. Browser profiles built for robots turned out to be holding the human's personal sessions at six vendors. And a cure for a known conflict class sat in quarantine for seven days while sync reverted fifty-six live scripts, the schedule watchdog among them, down one hundred ninety-seven lines.

Not one letter of any of those rules had changed. Each of them expired from a change in who it serves, and none of them stored which world it was written for.

The week's second rule: **a rule expires when its consumer changes, not when its text does.** Every rule needs a recorded "true while" clause, because the condition is visible at authoring time and invisible a month later.

The week has one counter-example so far, and it is the only boundary in either day that cannot expire. The write lease stopped trying to infer the write set of an arbitrary shell command and proved it cannot be done at all, then declared a categorical block instead of another allow-list entry. A boundary derived from the nature of the problem does not care who the consumer becomes.

This book stays inside its own sample, as usual. Day 110 was not written on its own morning: the book recorded zero commits on September 22, two days after a card named "the book does not watch its own output" was filed, and the gap recurred exactly where that card predicted. The source feeding the public-posts block is now stale for the fourth consecutive chapter, and Days 105, 106 and 107 still have sources on disk and no chapters.
