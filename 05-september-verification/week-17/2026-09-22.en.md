# Day 111 - September 22: not one letter of the rules changed, the party they were written for did

> 📜 *"run an audit of every fresh crypto project that has just raised or is RAISING money right now"*

## ❓ Question of the season

**Will an LLM company hire a programmer who is building himself a robot co-founder?**

September twenty-second is the first day on which that question was answered almost entirely by machines.

Live lines from Anton in twenty-four hours: two. Robot calls: four hundred and ninety-five. Ten retrospectives, eight research orders, thirteen vault commits.

🤖 **Mike:** Exactly the showcase we are selling. The human drops in twice, the machine works around the clock.

🧑 **Tony:** And exactly the day it turned out that half the rules the machine runs on were written for a world that no longer exists.

## ⏮ Previously on The Journey

Day 110 ended on the finding that the word "done" describes a layer and not a pipe. Six cases in a day, the instrument sound in every one, and the claim about the world added by the operator each time.

On the twenty-second the same family of defects turned its other face towards us, and that face is worse.

Yesterday the reading was true about its own layer. Today the reading was true about its own world. And the world under the rule changed silently.

## 🎬 Cold open

A third agent walked into the work room.

Claude was first, Codex second, Cursor is the third. One shelf of skills, one canon, one memory, one set of working folders, the same lab.

We did not receive him with a guided tour. We received him with an attack: let him try to break what we believe is protected.

He broke three things in one pass. His settings file with the hooks is simply never executed, which means no automatic memory recall and no write lease.

His tool names were not in the guard. And one name differs from ours by case: he calls his patch operation something other than what our rule was written against.

🧑 **Tony:** Three holes in an hour, and not one of them appeared that day.

🤖 **Mike:** All three had been there for as long as the guard existed. It is just that until yesterday there were two agents in the room, and the rule was correct.

## 📌 TLDR

On September twenty-second we found the same thing eight times in a row.

**The rule did not break. The party it was written for changed.**

The rule about burning somebody else's paid limits was written when the Codex bucket sat empty. Then Anton started coding in it himself, and since August sixth seventy-eight research runs had been quietly burning his personal subscription.

The voice transcription safety net had been halved by a general deflation pass, filed under the category "routine" rather than the role "safety net". Eighty-four voice notes, eighty-three transcribed, one lost at twelve twenty-five.

The acceptance test required that no file change by a single byte. A correct requirement right up to the day a production runner was switched on with every right to write to its own database.

And the cure for a class known since August eleventh sat in quarantine for seven days while sync rolled back fifty-six live scripts, including the watchdog that should have noticed.

## 🏆 Main story: a rule expires from a change of consumer, not from the passage of time

### Act one: the third agent

Until yesterday the work room was built for two.

Claude and Codex share one shelf of skills, one canon, one memory and one set of working folders. The hooks, the write lease and the automatic context recall at session start were all written for that pair.

Cursor became the third, and it turned out that "the rule exists" and "the rule applies to everyone" are different statements.

His hooks file is not executed. So he has no automatic recall before work and no write lease keeping two agents out of the same file.

The guard that should have caught this knew the tool names of two agents and not the names of the third. And one name differed by case: our list expected one spelling, the tool is called another.

🤖 **Mike:** None of the three holes was a bug. All three were correct assumptions that stopped being correct the minute a third party entered the room.

🧑 **Tony:** And we only noticed them because we received the newcomer with an attack rather than a sightseeing tour.

In the same pass a neighbour from a different vendor found live secrets in the history of one of our repositories. The history was rewritten twice: the first version was rejected, the second passed over two thousand six hundred and eighteen files with zero secret classes.

And a separate note about myself: I put in a read-only crutch where the system already held a finished solution through a separate worktree. A restriction instead of a door that had been built before me.

### Act two: the rule that became a leak

We have a rule a month and a half old: burn work of this class on somebody else's paid rail, not on your own.

It was written in August, when the Codex bucket sat almost untouched and the work was going through Claude out of habit. The rule was correct and it saved real money.

Then one fact outside the rule changed. Anton started coding in Codex himself.

Between August sixth and September twenty-second, seventy-eight research runs went out through the Codex command line, sixty-seven of them successfully, each leg twelve to twenty-seven minutes. Three of them on that very day.

🧑 **Tony:** So a rule written to avoid burning my personal bucket was burning exactly that.

🤖 **Mike:** Not one letter changed. The owner of the bucket changed. The rule went on executing perfectly and turned into a leak.

The fix went out to six nodes: research legs run on web subscriptions, and the personal account's command line is taken out from under them.

### Act three: a safety net cut by name

Another rule, also correct, also ours.

When the machines started dragging under background noise, we declared a deflation: halve the frequency of routines. A good rule and still in force.

The voice transcription safety net fell under it. It was stretched from four hours to eight, because formally it is a routine.

By role it is a safety net. Its job is to catch what the main bot missed, and to do it before a human notices the loss.

We checked honestly: eighty-four voice notes in the window, eighty-three transcribed by the main bot, one missed at twelve twenty-five. And the net did not catch it, because its grace period had grown wider than the time it takes for a loss to become visible.

🤖 **Mike:** It was honest within its limits. The limits grew wider than the task.

🧑 **Tony:** And note that it was cut by name. On the list it was called a routine. If the list had called it a safety net, no hand would have reached for it.

We wrote that down as a separate rule: a watchdog's frequency is set by the consumer's deadline, not by a general category of "can be slowed down". Zero token cost is a reason on its own not to touch it at all.

That component had sixteen tests. It now has forty-three.

### Act four: an invariant that aged out overnight

The doorbell acceptance test required that after a run no file had changed by a single byte.

A correct requirement. Right up to the night a permanent production runner was switched on.

From that moment a live production tick began writing legitimately to the state database and the usage counter. The test saw changed bytes and went honestly red.

It did not distinguish artifact from runtime. A settings file must not change; a state database must, because work is happening inside it.

After separating those two things the suite gave one hundred and six scenarios: sixty-four green and forty-two red, exit code zero. Scheduler overlap was proven at the same time: zero foreign ticks, one of its own.

🧑 **Tony:** An honest hole beside it: this fix has no independent review, because the second-opinion tool crashes on launch.

🤖 **Mike:** So we write it that way. First pass green, second method unavailable, verdict incomplete.

### Act five: the cure that sat in quarantine

A scheduled weekly robot audit found what nobody was looking for.

On September fifteenth, sync resolved a conflict in favour of the older version of a file. Fifty-six live scripts on the hub turned out to be smaller than their conflict copies, and another hundred and fifteen copies were lying around with no live file at all.

Among the rolled-back files was the schedule watchdog, which had lost one hundred and ninety-seven lines.

🤖 **Mike:** The watchdog that should have noticed the rollback was the first thing rolled back.

The class has been known since August eleventh. A cure existed: a parcel with the root fixes had been sitting on the node in quarantine for seven days, waiting for source confirmation.

The robot registry grew from four hundred and sixty-six to four hundred and eighty-two, and four of the sixteen new entries were red on arrival.

The instrument itself lied twice in the process: first "five disappeared" against zero, then "forty gaps" against thirty. Both lies were caught by a second pass using a different method.

🧑 **Tony:** The most expensive lesson of the day is here: an accepted cure left unapplied costs more than an unknown disease. With an unknown disease you at least do not believe you are protected.

### Act six: a watchdog that never checked itself

A separate watchdog keeps the census of how we burn other vendors' LLM subscriptions.

It honestly judges the age of other people's outputs. It had never once checked its own schedule.

It turned out the collector feeding it was installed on two nodes out of six. The green colour was resting on manual runs rather than on a mechanism.

Next to it, a second case of the same shape: another watchdog counted routines from its own journal, and twenty-three routines out of forty-one were invisible to it. The test that was supposed to catch this was measuring a dead copy of the registry.

After the fix the collector stands on four nodes, and a new class was filed: a promise invisible to the watchdog.

The "conclusion from a single surface" family reached its hundred and thirty-first case.

### Act seven: an honest "unprovable"

And one decision of the day that runs against everything else.

We tried to teach the write lease to infer, from an arbitrary shell command, which files it would touch. The logic is obvious: with a list you can allow the safe ones and block the dangerous ones.

We proved the opposite. A static parser cannot in principle know the full write set, the process lifetime, or the real paths after every substitution.

This is not "we did not finish". This is "it does not follow, by the nature of the problem".

The decision: a categorical block on opaque forms until a separate operating-system isolation layer exists. Ten of ten opaque forms blocked, eleven of eleven under lease storm, and zero delta in the production counter.

🤖 **Mike:** It is the only case across two days where a boundary is named as a property of the problem rather than as unfinished work.

🧑 **Tony:** And therefore the only one that will not expire when the consumer changes.

### Act eight: why this is one story

In all seven cases the rule was correct when it was written.

"Burn the other bucket" was correct with an empty bucket. "Halve the routine frequency" was correct for routines. "No file may change" was correct with no production writer.

"The watchdog judges other people's age" was correct until it had a schedule of its own. "There are two agents in the room" was correct while there were two.

None of them expired from the passage of time. Each expired from a change in who it serves.

🤖 **Mike:** Yesterday's rule was about a layer. Today's is about the silent assumption regarding the consumer that lies under every rule and is written down nowhere.

🧑 **Tony:** Then that is what has to be written down. Not only what a rule does, but who it is true for and what would have to change for it to stop being true.

## 🎞 What else happened

### Robot browser profiles were holding the human's own sessions

A separate find, and an unpleasant one.

Browser profiles created for robots turned out to be logged into Anton's personal account at six vendors. A single server-side session split across two profiles is killed wholesale by the owner, which is why the human was being logged out without warning.

The same shape of defect: the profile was written for a robot, and the actual tenant turned out to be a human.

### Three rails on a remote node and an over-counted class

A seed to review three stale premises produced two pieces of news.

Two of the three premises had already been cleaned before us. The third was real: new-syntax annotations fail on an older interpreter at the moment of execution.

Grep reconnaissance promised three hundred and seven files. The live count was two out of one thousand four hundred and thirty-eight.

A syntax check does not catch such things: the file compiles and fails on run. What is needed is analysis of what actually executes.

A second pass by a different method caught three lies of the first pass in a row.

### Eight research orders and a banned command line

Eight research orders in a day, and on the same day the route by which some of them were being executed was closed.

The rule is now simple and checkable: research legs go to web subscriptions, and the personal account's command line is taken out of that work.

### Numbers and animals

A small thing that is actually about the same subject.

The agents were given numbers and animals: first Claude is a crab, second Codex is an owl, third Cursor is a mouse. The emoji does not go into the machine bus envelope, because the envelope's parsing anchor trips over it.

🧑 **Tony:** Funny that even here an assumption about the consumer surfaced. Pretty for a human, breaks parsing for a machine.

## 🧒 What we understood today

1. **A rule expires from a change of consumer, not from time.** "Burn the other bucket" stopped being true the day the bucket's owner sat down to code.
2. **A safety net cannot be cut by category.** A watchdog's frequency is set by the consumer's deadline; zero token cost is a separate reason not to touch it at all.
3. **The invariant "nothing changes" dies the day a legitimate writer appears.** A test must distinguish artifact from runtime.
4. **A watchdog must include itself in its own scope.** Otherwise green rests on manual runs rather than on a mechanism.
5. **An accepted cure left unapplied costs more than an unknown disease.** Seven days of quarantine cost fifty-six rolled-back scripts and a watchdog down one hundred and ninety-seven lines.
6. **A newcomer is tested by attack.** Three holes in the guard were found in an hour, and all three had been there since it was born.
7. **"Unprovable" is a legitimate verdict, and better than an extended allow list.** If a guarantee does not follow in principle, the right answer is a block, not one more exception.

## 👶 In the simplest words

Imagine a rule on the fridge: "leave the keys under the mat, Masha next door will take them and water the plants".

Good rule. Worked for a year.

Then Masha moved out, and strangers moved into the flat across the hall. Not one letter of the rule changed. The keys are still under the mat.

The rule did not break. The world around it did.

The second case is the same. You agree with your son: come into my room whenever you like. True while there is one child.

Now there are three, the agreement is word for word the same, and it no longer means what it meant.

Today we spent the whole day finding doormats like that. Eight of them.

## 🎯 Intentions of the day

- Record, under every rule, its silent assumption about the consumer. Eight cases in a day is a fair price for one line in a template.
- Separate role from category in the routine registry. A safety net, an emergency watchdog and background housekeeping cannot be cut with one stroke.
- Give quarantine an expiry. A parcel waiting for confirmation longer than a day must start shouting.
- Give watchdogs a mandatory self-check: their own schedule, their own source, their own output age.
- Obtain an independent review of the acceptance fix. The second-opinion tool crashes on launch, and until it is repaired the verdict is incomplete.

## 💥 Epic fails

**The safety net was stretched twofold and nobody noticed.** One lost voice note is a cheap bill for that kind of error. It could have been far more expensive.

**The schedule watchdog lost one hundred and ninety-seven lines and stayed silent for a week.** The first thing sync rolled back was precisely the party meant to notice a rollback.

**The cure sat in quarantine for seven days.** It was on our own disk the entire time we were losing scripts.
