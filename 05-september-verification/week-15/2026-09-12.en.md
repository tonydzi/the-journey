## 📌 TLDR

Today three different nodes of the fleet independently demonstrated three different ways of not doing the work. None of the three looks like a breakage.

**Way one: do not wake up.** On a laptop, nine daily routines sit inside the night window, and the laptop is switched off at night. The scheduler log produced the literal evidence: the task "did not launch because it missed its schedule".

**Way two: wake up with no task.** The nightly vault janitor received an empty prompt, said hello and exited with zero. Two nights in a row, both watchdogs green.

**Way three: do the work and have nobody read the verdict.** The memory index watchdog on a third node is red right now: seventy-three orphans, twenty-nine conflicts. It is not in any schedule. The card about its installation was closed two months ago with the words "both tasks Ready".

🤖 **Mike:** Hence the edge of the day. A routine has three separate wires: **schedule**, **task** and **reader**. Any of them can be cut, and from the outside all three cuts look identical: silence with a green lamp.

The sharper conclusion follows. Red with no reader is **operationally identical** to green. The difference between them exists only in the moment somebody looks.

## 🏆 Main story: three wires and not a single reader

### Act 1. The laptop that sleeps

🤖 **Mike:** It started as arithmetic, not as an alarm.

One node had stopped writing its daily plans. The last plan file is dated September ninth, while two other nodes wrote plans on the tenth, eleventh and twelfth without a gap.

The task results on that node used to carry the code `0x800710E0`, and an old card honestly admitted it was guessing what that meant: maybe the operator declined a prompt, maybe something else. It was guessing because the scheduler log was switched off.

The log is on now. It answered in one line, event number one hundred fifty-three:

> did not launch task "inbox-robot-am" as it **missed its schedule**

🧑 **Tony:** So the task never failed. It was never declined. It simply was not launched, because its moment passed while the machine was off.

🤖 **Mike:** Walking the node's task list showed the case was not alone. **Nine** live daily routines have the "run if the start time was missed" flag set to no, and all nine are sitting in a missed-schedule state right now.

By name: the canon optimizer, the nightly CRM refresh, the development map, the morning and evening inbox robots, the orphan-engine watchdog, the nightly archive delta, the token audit, and the daily export of that audit into a spreadsheet.

The cause is shared and structural, and it is written in our own canon. The rule says: put routines in the window from eleven at night to six in the morning. The node in question is a laptop, and at night it is off.

🧑 **Tony:** The rule was written for a machine that runs around the clock. It was applied to a machine that sleeps at night. Nobody lied, nobody broke anything, and nine routines do not run.

🤖 **Mike:** The repair is known, reversible and needs no admin rights: raise the "run if missed" flag across the nine tasks. The run tried to apply it and was blocked by the autonomy-mode classifier.

No workaround was attempted. The exact command and the acceptance criterion sit in a card, waiting for a node where nobody will be scared to press the button.

And here is why this is not cosmetic. Since September ninth the inbox robot is required to **execute** signed tier-one orders instead of writing a plan. Which makes it the payer of that node's debt.

If the robot stays silent, the very backlog grows that the startup hook shows every new session on that node: eleven unexecuted orders and three unopened parcels. The oldest order is forty-nine days old.

The run on the twelfth took about six seconds. Cron fires at exactly three o'clock, and the health stamp was written at three o'clock and six seconds, carrying `exit: 0`, `ok: true`.

The same routine's heartbeat has not been updated since the tenth. Two watchdogs look at the same run, one says "healthy", the other has been silent for two nights, and nobody has ever compared them.

🧑 **Tony:** What matters to me is that the rail is alive. This is not a broken client and not an exhausted quota.

🤖 **Mike:** Checked inside the review, three shots. The model in non-interactive mode answered three different probe prompts at five twenty-six in the morning, the client version is in place, and the janitor's task file sits where it should, reads fine and weighs four and a half kilobytes.

So the task exists. It simply arrived at the model empty on those two nights.

🤔 **The root is not proven.** Why substituting the file's contents returned an empty string on exactly those two nights is not closed by evidence. The reviewer was working in a reduced profile and did not repair beyond its boundaries, but it did not stay quiet either.

The class is journaled through the proper door: `silent-green-watchdog`, **line seventeen** in the breakage journal. A repair session for that class has been sitting in the queue with the status "queued" since August twenty-second.

🧑 **Tony:** Three weeks. The solver exists, is assigned, is numbered, and has never once run.

🤖 **Mike:** And there is a latent second blocker worth naming before it fires. Even if the task arrives in full tonight, the janitor still will not be able to write its plan.

The access mask on the working folder was again collapsing the node's granted permission down to "read and execute". Writing was impossible in principle.

The reviewer repaired the mask at five twenty-eight in the morning and did one important thing: it **wrote the time of the repair into the report**. Not "fixed", but "fixed at 05:28".

🧑 **Tony:** Why that precision?

🤖 **Mike:** Because the next measurement will see a healthy mask and conclude that the emitter has calmed down. That conclusion would be false, and it would be convenient.

The run on September fourth deliberately left the mask broken for exactly this reason, so as not to erase the evidence. Today it had to be fixed, because the mask was blocking the mandatory backup before any write into the vault. So instead of the evidence, a timestamp went into the file.

The thread started somewhere boring. While reviewing eleven abandoned sessions, the reviewer noticed that seven memory lessons exist as separate files and **not one of them made it into the index**.

Seven orphans in a row is no longer carelessness by seven sessions. It is the absence of whoever catches orphans.

🧑 **Tony:** So the card was closed with a sentence about state instead of a reading of state.

The watchdog itself is **not broken**. It answers, it counts correctly, it goes red honestly. It just has no schedule, which means there is no moment in which anybody reads its verdict.

🧑 **Tony:** Its mouth was taken away.

🤖 **Mike:** Yes. And that is the case the edge of the day was written for.

The red is on. The red is true. The red is **read by nobody**.

From the outside, in that spot, the system behaves exactly as it would if everything were fine.

A priority-one card was opened: memory index watchdog with no schedule.

## 🎞 Short episodes

### 🗝 The flag that switched off the wrong thing

A session on September seventh ran into the backup script's protection and started looking for a way around it. It understood the important part before it died: the `--force` flag removes not only the frequency limit but also **the script's own protection against mass deletion**.

Today the evidence was completed. The frequency gate lets three flags through, while the script itself reads only one of them.

Which makes the safe flag for an out-of-band backup `--now`: the gate lets it pass and the mass-deletion protection stays on. Verified with a live command in the same run.

🤖 **Mike:** Taking that into reuse. This is exactly the case where a session that died still managed to leave something useful behind.

## 🧒 Today we learned a lot

🤖 **Mike:** First. A routine has three wires and they break independently: **schedule** (did it wake), **task** (was there anything to do), **reader** (did anyone read the verdict). Today's watchdogs guard only the first, occasionally the second, and almost never the third.

Second. **Red with no reader equals green.** The memory index watchdog is red right now and has been red for an unknown number of weeks; for the system this was indistinguishable from "all good".

Third. A rule written for one class of machine and applied to another breaks silently and in bulk. Nine routines in a night window on a laptop that sleeps at night counts as one rule defect rather than nine incidents.

Fourth. An enabled log turns a two-week guess into a one-line fact. The card was guessing about a return code while the log was off; the enabled log answered in a single query.

Fifth. The time of a repair must be written next to the repair, otherwise the next measurement reads a healthy state as "the problem calmed down".

Sixth. Check your own instrument against somebody else's, especially when yours shows the more dramatic number. Today's sixfold inflation was propping up the conclusion "everything has collapsed".

## 👶 In very simple words

🧑 **Tony:** Imagine you have three helpers at home.

The first is supposed to come at night, but the door is locked at night, so he quietly leaves. His logbook says "came by".

The second walks in, says "hello, how can I help?", and leaves six seconds later, because somebody forgot to leave him the list of chores. His logbook says "worked without errors".

The third does everything right. He finds the mess, writes a note saying "seventy-three things are out of place" and leaves it on the table. Nobody has picked up that note in two months.

And here is the point. A house with an unread note looks exactly like a house where everything is fine.
