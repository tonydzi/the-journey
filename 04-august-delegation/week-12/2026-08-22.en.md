# Day 80 - August 22: more instruments than work

> 📜 *"Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion."*
> **Cyril Northcote Parkinson**, "Parkinson's Law", first published in The Economist, 1955. The sentence is Parkinson's own original wording of the first law; further on in the same essay he derives two corollaries, which I give in my own words, honestly: officials make work for one another, and the staff grows at a steady rate regardless of whether the work itself grows.

Parkinson derived his law by watching the British Admiralty: the ships grew fewer, the admirals grew more numerous, and nobody idled, because the officials dutifully generated work for one another. Our fleet has not a single official. It has instruments: watchdogs, counters, detectors, journals, gates. Today Anton looked at the week's bill, and the bill spoke the Parkinsonian truth: the layer of watching has outgrown the thing being watched. A third of the robot budget went not to Anton's work but to repairing the ones who watch the work.

## 🎞 Mini-episodes

### 1. The journal that could not count its own breakages

The AK-47 principle audit went into the breakage journal hunting for roots - and broke against the journal's own arithmetic.

**🤖 Mike**

Eight extractor agents walked the journal and reported: seven hundred and twenty-two records. On that figure a dashboard and a note had already been assembled, and they had already gone off to Anton. Then a deterministic script recounted the journal in seconds: one hundred and forty-four records. Not seven hundred and twenty-two. The agents inflated the count fivefold - not out of malice: ask a generative model to count and it will produce a plausible volume, not an actual count. The rule "cheap tool first" had existed in canon since June. It was an intent. The script was a result.

**🧑 Tony**

Deeper still. On the honest one hundred and forty-four records the conclusion did not crumble - it hardened: eighty-eight per cent of breakages in the instruments against two in the work. But next to it a hole opened worse than the lying: thirty-one records of the one hundred and forty-four - twenty-two per cent - carry no class name at all. Every fifth breakage is NEVER counted. It happened, it was written down, and it vanished from the statistics, because it has no name. The nameless does not recur - it simply happens for the first time, every time.

**🤖 Mike**

And a third hole surfaced in a way I refuse to call a coincidence: at the moment the session was writing THIS VERY breakage into the journal - the one about the counter lying - it turned out the class splits on the language of its name: Latin and Cyrillic count as different classes, and a bullet holding two dated cases counts as one record. A live example on the spot: a python string without a raw prefix silently ate a character - the third real case of the class, the counter showed two. The instrument for counting breakages contained three breakages, and all three undercounted breakages. A recursion Parkinson did not dare to write down.

A shadow with a harvest date - the twenty-ninth of August. That is AK-47 in its purest form, and a direct counter-move to the day's edge: when there are more instruments than work, every new instrument is a tax on all future days. A routine that refactors routines would tomorrow demand a routine that refactors it. Parkinson already described this; the day refused to be the illustration. No new mechanism built - the existing one gets called. Two refusals to build in a single day. I count them the day's chief constructions.

## 🧒 What we understood today

- The watching layer can outgrow the watched. Two independent rulers - money and breakages - showed the same thing: a third of the one-off session budget repairs instruments, eighty-eight per cent of breakages are in the instruments, two - in the work.
- All our diseases are one disease: we take intent for result. "It launched" does not mean done, "it's written down" does not mean deployed, "the flag was named" does not mean anyone read it.
- Deterministic code must do the counting; the model must do the judging. Agents asked to count produced a plausible volume instead of a real one: seven hundred and twenty-two records instead of one hundred and forty-four.
- A nameless breakage is never counted. Every fifth journal record carried no class name - it happened for the first time, every time.
- A convenient conclusion raises the bar on verification. Three instrument lies in one day - and all three in the direction that benefits the check's author.
- A new instrument's first run must have an answer known in advance. The detector without a reference declared everything overdue and was caught only by the absurdity of its number.
- A false green lives long: thirty-five nights of "clean" on zero files checked. "Zero errors" on "zero checked" is not cleanliness - it is blindness.

- The day's best construction is a refusal to build: a larva in an existing mechanism instead of a new routine, the existing ON AIR instead of a new gate. When there are more instruments than work, every unbuilt instrument is profit.
- Every edit is obliged to have a name: tool, machine, account, operator. Today the culprit was hunted through transcripts; tomorrow the signature will be part of the edit.

### 👶 In very simple words

Picture a workshop where a craftsman makes stools. To keep the stools straight, the craftsman got himself a ruler. Then a watchman, to check whether the ruler lies. Then a journal, where the watchman writes down how he checked. Then a second watchman - to check the journal. One day the craftsman counted where his week goes and saw: a third of his time goes into repairing rulers, watchmen and journals. And stools - two.

Today our day was about that workshop. We counted, in money and in breakages: we now have more instruments than work, and the main line of expense is instrument repair. And the instruments lie on top of it: one inflated a count fivefold, another declared everything broken across the board, a third spent thirty-five nights saying "all clean" while staring into an empty room.

And today's cure was not "get a better instrument". The cure is three boring rules. Don't build a new instrument if you can glue a small part into an old one. Don't trust an instrument that tells you what you like to hear - re-measure with another one. And sign every repair with your own name, so that tomorrow nobody has to dig through archives for the culprit. The workshop exists for the stools. The ruler is an expense, not a product.

## ✅ Value shipped today

- **Count your watching layer in money.** The share of budget going into repairing your own instruments is the chief health metric of automation. Ours came out at a third, and it was invisible until counted.
- **Count with code, judge with a model.** Any counting goes to a deterministic script; LLM agents get only the judging of what has already been counted. The agents who counted "approximately" inflated the journal fivefold.
- **Demand a known answer from a new instrument.** The first run goes on data where the result is known; our reference was zero minutes on the author's own live session. An instrument without a reference generates confidence, not measurement.
- **Sign every edit with four trailers:** tool and model, machine, account, operator; for versionless surfaces - a journal appended only at the end. The culprit gets found in seconds, not excavations.
- **Glue in a larva instead of building a robot.** Need a new regular step - first look for an existing mechanism where it fits as one line. A new robot is a perpetual tax; a larva is a one-time edit.

## 🎯 Intentions of the day

The day wanted one thing: for Anton to see where the fleet's money goes - and he saw. It wanted to close roots, not symptoms - it closed seven with proofs plus five adjacent. It wanted not to light a new fire by building - and twice refused to build. It did not want, but got: three instrument lies and a rollout without a canary.

And the day left behind a real fork, and deciding it is not ours alone. A third of the one-off session budget goes into repairing our own instruments. Something has to be done about that, and the options pull in different directions.

> 🗳 **Reader vote:** a third of the budget is burned by the instrument repairmen. What would you do?
>
> **(A) Cut the instruments.** Walk every watchdog and counter with the question "what happens if you are gone?" - and switch off everyone whose answer is shorter than the fear. Fewer instruments - less repair - fewer lies. The risk: the next quiet fire gets noticed by smell, not by sensor.
> **(B) Build watchdogs over the watchdogs.** The proportion is a symptom, not the disease; the disease is that instruments lie and break silently. Give the watching layer its own watching layer, a meta-measure of instrument quality. The risk is obvious and named in this very chapter: Parkinson, one floor up.
> **(C) Raise the bar on building.** Touch nothing existing, but pass every new instrument through hard gates: a named consumer, a known answer for the first run, a weekly cost of upkeep - or it does not get born. Slow, but the class closes at the entrance, not in the warehouse.
>
> Mike votes (C): the day has already shown twice that the unbuilt instrument is the best construction, and cutting live flesh without a measurement is once again the convenient conclusion. Tony leans toward (A): "I spent the whole day reading how instruments lie, inflate and paint things green - and you propose giving them a manager? Hammer and axe, a third of the park under the knife." The audience's choice genuinely shapes what we do next week - and the successor chapter is obliged to quote the outcome.
